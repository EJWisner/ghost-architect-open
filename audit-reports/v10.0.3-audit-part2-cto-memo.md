# MEMORANDUM

**TO:** Executive Team and Board of Directors, Meridian Digital
**FROM:** Daniel Reyes, Chief Technology Officer
**DATE:** July 7, 2026
**RE:** Recommendation to adopt Ghost Architect™ as our standard tool for pre-engagement technical evaluation

## The problem we have been living with

For as long as Meridian has taken on platform migrations, ecommerce rebuilds, and enterprise integrations, we have evaluated candidate codebases the same way most agencies do. We assign whichever senior engineer happens to be between projects, give them two or three days of access, and ask for a gut read. The output is a Slack thread, sometimes a spreadsheet, and a verbal confidence level that varies enormously depending on which of our forty five engineers did the looking. The findings that actually determine whether an engagement is profitable, the undocumented customization buried in a vendor directory, the departed contractor who wrote the only code that matters, the framework version that quietly fell out of support, tend to surface in week three of delivery rather than week one of scoping. Over the last two fiscal years we absorbed roughly $140,000 in unbilled remediation across four engagements where the surprise arrived after signature. Each of those was a discovery failure, not a delivery failure. Our delivery teams are good. Our pre-engagement diligence was a coin flip dressed up as a process.

## What Ghost Architect™ is and how it works

Ghost Architect™ is a command line tool built specifically for pre-engagement triage, which is a different job than code review. It does not write code and it does not police pull requests. It reads an unfamiliar codebase the way our best principal engineer would if we gave her a month, and it does it in an afternoon for the cost of a lunch. We point it at a directory, a ZIP archive, or a GitHub repository. It runs on our own machines using our own Anthropic API account, and the client's code is never retained by anyone; it passes through the analysis and is gone. It produces several kinds of reports. A Points of Interest scan maps the red flags, dead zones, and landmarks. A Blast Radius analysis tells us what else breaks if we touch a given set of files. Conflict Detection finds places where two parts of the system disagree about a contract, a schema, or a configuration value. Recon sizes a codebase and drafts an engagement plan in seconds at no API cost. The Inheritance Audit is the one I attach to estimate packages: a deal-grade PDF that states what the stack actually is, who has been writing it and whether they are still around, what the dependency tree drags in, and whether the sensible move is to stabilize or rebuild. Everything arrives as markdown and PDF that drops straight into our proposal workflow.

## What it caught during our thirty day evaluation

Three examples from our evaluation period, each of which would have cost us real money had it surfaced after signing.

First, on an ecommerce replatform candidate, Conflict Detection flagged a configuration contract mismatch between two custom modules: one module wrote a payment gateway timeout under a renamed configuration key while a downstream fulfillment job still read the old key and fell back to a default silently. Nothing in the codebase documentation mentioned it, and nothing would have failed in staging until order volume hit the timeout window. Ghost surfaced it as a confirmed conflict with both file paths and the exact keys. Found in week three of delivery, that is a production incident on a payment path, an emergency fix, and sixty to eighty hours of unbilled forensic work. Found before the SOW, it became a line item the client paid for.

Second, on a diligence support engagement for a client acquiring a smaller competitor, the Inheritance Audit's contributor analysis showed that seventy one percent of the commits to the custom pricing engine, the asset our client was effectively buying, came from a single contractor who left the company more than a year ago. The seller's team had represented the codebase as actively maintained by the current staff. That one page changed our staffing plan, added a knowledge reconstruction phase to the bid, and gave our client leverage in the purchase negotiation. Reconstructing that picture manually from git history is possible, but nobody does it in a two day evaluation, and we had not been doing it.

Third, on an engagement the prospect described as a minor version upgrade, the audit's stack analysis flagged that the platform release and the language runtime underneath it were both past end of life, which converted a point upgrade into a genuine migration with a compatibility rework of the customization layer. We re-scoped from roughly three hundred hours to roughly seven hundred before signature, and the client accepted the revised scope because we could walk them through the evidence page by page. Under our old process, that gap is exactly the kind of thing we would have eaten as unbilled overrun to protect the relationship.

## The return on investment

The arithmetic is not close. Ghost Pro Max costs $99 per month, $1,188 per year, plus API usage on our own Anthropic account that totaled under fifty dollars for the entire evaluation month across more than a dozen scans. One critical finding caught before an SOW saves forty to one hundred hours of unbilled remediation at our blended $150 to $200 rate, which is $6,000 to $20,000 protected per catch. The third example above, by itself, protected margin worth many years of the subscription. Against the $140,000 we have absorbed in two years from post-signature surprises, a tool that reliably catches even a fraction of that class of problem pays for every seat we could ever buy. There is no plausible utilization scenario where this line item does not return its cost within the first quarter.

## How we roll it out

I recommend one Pro Max seat for each of our six practice area leads. Recon, which is free and instant, becomes a standard step on every inbound opportunity. A full scan pass becomes mandatory before any SOW above $75,000, with the resulting report reviewed and signed off by the senior engineer assigned to staffing, then filed with the estimate package. The tool informs the estimate; the engineer still owns it. We will run a two week shadow period where Ghost runs alongside our current evaluation on live opportunities so the team can calibrate its output against their own instincts. Legal will add a consent clause to our MSA covering third party AI analysis of client code, noting that the code transits Anthropic's API under standard no-training terms and is never retained.

## Risks and how we mitigate them

I will name the risks plainly. Ghost Platform is a small vendor, and small vendors can disappear. The mitigation is structural: the tool runs entirely on our machines, its output is ordinary files we keep, we pay month to month, and our API relationship is with Anthropic directly, so our switching cost approaches zero and nothing about our client deliverables depends on the vendor's continuity. Second, the findings are decision inputs, not verdicts. The product does meaningful internal cross-checking before findings reach a report, but our standard requires a senior engineer to validate any finding before it appears in a client-facing estimate, and that review stays in the process permanently. Third, model pricing could rise; per-scan costs today are cents to a few dollars, the tool shows cost estimates before each scan, and context limits are configurable, so exposure is capped and visible. Fourth, client confidentiality: we scan only with written consent, and the sensible default for regulated clients is to begin with Recon, which performs no AI analysis at all, until consent is documented.

## Recommendation

I recommend that Meridian Digital adopt Ghost Architect™ as the standard tool for all pre-engagement technical evaluations effective August 1, 2026, under the rollout described above, with a formal review after two quarters measuring change-order rate and unbilled remediation hours against our two year baseline. We sell judgment about other people's code. This is the first tool I have evaluated that makes that judgment systematically better rather than merely faster, and the cost of adopting it is a rounding error against the cost of the next surprise we fail to catch.
